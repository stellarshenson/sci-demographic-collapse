# [Paper digest] Using Geographic Distance as a Potential Proxy for Help in the Assessment of the Grandmother Hypothesis

**Authors**: Sacha C. Engelhardt, Patrick Bergeron, Alain Gagnon, Lisa Dillon, Fanie Pelletier<br>
**Year**: 2019  **Venue**: Current Biology 29(4):651-656<br>
**Original (link)**: [https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(19)30029-6](https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(19)30029-6) (DOI 10.1016/j.cub.2018.12.026)<br>
**Local PDF**: none - **paywalled** (Cell Press); digest-only from abstract and published figures<br>
**Used in**: E18-H157 (kin proximity / grandmother childcare supports fertility)

## Summary

Tests the grandmother hypothesis using geographic distance between mother and maternal grandmother as a proxy for the availability of grandmaternal help, in the St. Lawrence Valley population (17th-18th century), controlling for shared genetic and environmental effects.

## Key findings

- A living maternal grandmother raised her daughter's number of children born by ~2.1 and children surviving to age 15 by ~1.1 (versus a deceased grandmother)
- The benefit decays with distance: over the 0-325 km range, greater mother-grandmother distance lowered the number of offspring born and lifetime reproductive success, and raised age at first reproduction - even with the grandmother alive
- Proximity, not merely survival, drives the effect: help must be logistically deliverable to matter
- Consistent with cooperative-breeding / kin-support mechanisms for human fertility

## Relevance to this project

Grounds H157: grandmaternal (and more broadly kin) proximity is a real fertility input operating through childcare help, and it attenuates with distance. Modern residential dispersion and delayed childbearing weaken this support channel, giving a kin-network undercurrent to low fertility that cash transfers do not address - and pointing to childcare availability, not only money, as the operative lever.
