## Key mechanism

Establishes the local male-earnings shock as an identification design. The Appalachian coal boom of the 1970s and bust of the 1980s (Kentucky, Ohio, Pennsylvania, West Virginia) delivered a large, exogenous, male-skewed swing in local earnings, letting the authors trace how a mining-sector shock propagates into the rest of a local economy. This is the foundational earnings-shock template that later marriageable-men work (Kearney-Wilson's fracking study, the companion Black et al. household-structure paper) builds on to link male earnings to demography.

## Main findings

- Employment spillovers into local-goods sectors are modest and asymmetric: one mining job created in the boom generates ~0.174 local-sector jobs, while one mining job lost in the bust destroys ~0.349 - the bust hurts more than the boom helps
- No positive spillovers into traded-goods sectors (goods sold nationally), and little evidence of negative spillovers either
- The coal boom reduced out-migration of prime-aged men and drew return-migration of men who had previously left - the earnings shock directly reshaped the local prime-age male population
- Mining earnings rose steadily 1969-1977 then fell in the bust; earnings-per-worker effects in local sectors were small and often not statistically significant, so the shock ran mainly through employment and population, not local wage levels

## Key takeaways

- A resource-driven local earnings shock is a valid natural experiment for male economic prospects - the design template behind the marriageable-men literature
- Local labour-market shocks are asymmetric and only partly propagate: booms create few spillover jobs, busts destroy more, and traded-goods sectors are insulated
- Because the shock changed the size and composition of the prime-age male population (migration), it is a plausible upstream driver of local marriage and fertility - the demographic reading is developed in the companion Black et al. work that Kearney-Wilson cite for the coal-era marriage/birth response
