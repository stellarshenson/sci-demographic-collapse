## Summary

The paper documents that admission to selective US colleges became sharply more competitive from roughly the 1970s to the 2000s, and that students responded by *over-investing* in signals of ability - more applications, more standardized-test-taking, more test-prep - without commensurate gains in what they actually learn. This is the empirical anatomy of a **positional arms race**: what matters for the payoff is a student's *rank* relative to other applicants, not the absolute skill acquired, so rising competition pulls everyone to invest more just to hold position. The authors find little evidence that the extra effort produced long-term learning benefits, and some suggestive evidence the reverse. For the demographic model this is the canonical microfoundation for treating education as a partly zero-sum positional good whose escalating cost (money and years) crowds out family formation - the mechanism behind Korea's hagwon arms race and the campaign's dE credential-competition term.

## Parameters for the model

- **Window**: US high-school cohorts ~**1972 -> 2004** (NLS72, HSB82, NELS92, ELS04 longitudinal surveys); institutional data 1986-2003
- **Demand surge**: applicants to 4-year colleges grew **1.19M -> 1.71M (1992-2004), +44%**; share of students applying to a 4-year college rose from **29% (1972) to 53% (2004)**
- **Supply frozen (the positional squeeze)**: top-20 private universities + top-20 liberal-arts colleges changed average undergrad enrollment by only **+0.7% (1986-2003)**; fewer than **500,000** slots added system-wide 1992-2004 while demand rose -> selectivity rises purely from the rank race
- **Selectivity rise**: acceptance rates fell about a **quarter** at the top-20 private and top-20 public universities; national 4-year acceptance-probability for a fixed-ability applicant fell **~9%** (1972->2004)
- **Signal inflation**: 75th-percentile math SAT at top-20 public universities rose **+52 points (+8.1%)**, 1986-2003; top-20 privates **+41 points** (to 749) - qualifications ratchet up to hold the same position
- **Over-investment with no learning payoff**: cross-region comparisons show "little evidence" that intensified competition raised what students learn - effort is spent on positional signalling, not human capital

## Model wiring

- Treat the education return as **relative/positional**, not absolute: model a credential-arms-race term dE where an individual's payoff depends on rank in the applicant distribution, so unilateral disinvestment is penalised and the equilibrium over-invests (money + years)
- The frozen elite supply (+0.7%) with surging demand (+44%) is the parameterisation of a fixed positional good -> escalating cost with zero aggregate capacity gain; this is the cost that competes with family formation (couples to Korea's edupoverty / hagwon spend already in the campaign, E15-H88/89)
- "No long-term learning benefit" justifies modelling a share of education spending as pure signalling deadweight - a lever that can be capped without losing human capital (supports lottery-band / de-determinized admission interventions)
- Supplies the "geometry over magnitude" prior: because the good is positional, blanket subsidies to education just inflate the race; only changing the *rules* (rank compression, lottery) relieves it

## Caveats

- US selective-college context; the *positional* mechanism generalises but the specific magnitudes do not transfer to Korea/Japan tracking systems - use as a mechanism prior, not a country calibration
- Documents behaviour and selectivity, not a direct education->fertility coefficient; the fertility link is via cost/time crowd-out, established elsewhere in the campaign
- Learning-effect evidence is "limited" and suggestive by the authors' own hedging - treat the deadweight-signalling share as a modelling assumption, not a measured fraction
