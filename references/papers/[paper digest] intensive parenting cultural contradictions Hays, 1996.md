## Key mechanism

Hays names the dominant ideology of contemporary child-rearing INTENSIVE MOTHERING: parenting that is child-centred, expert-guided, emotionally absorbing, labour-intensive and financially expensive, with the mother (and now, per Ishizuka, the father too) responsible for cultivating the child. The "cultural contradiction" is that this anti-market devotion is demanded inside a society that otherwise prizes competitive self-interest, so the bar for an adequate parent is set impossibly high and pulls against paid work. The fertility-relevant mechanism is a rising PARENTING BAR: as the required investment per child climbs, the perceived cost and the fear of not being "good enough" rise, raising the effective price of each child. Ishizuka supplies the quantitative confirmation that this bar is now normative across social classes and both parent genders.

## Main findings

- Hays: the hegemonic norm is intensive mothering - child-centred, expert-driven, emotionally consuming, time- and money-intensive; contradictory because it demands anti-market devotion within a market society
- Ishizuka (N ≈ 3,642 US parents, nationally representative vignette experiment): "concerted cultivation" (intensive parenting) is rated the standard of good parenting far above "natural growth" (independent play, minimal supervision)
- ~75% of BOTH college-graduate and non-college-graduate parents rated the intensive vignette "very good" or "excellent" - the standard is shared across class
- Only 32% of college graduates and 38% of non-college graduates rated the low-intensity vignette positively - less-intensive parenting is broadly judged inadequate
- The standard holds for both MOTHERS and FATHERS and for sons and daughters alike - a general parenting norm, not gender- or child-sex-specific
- Lower-income parents endorse the same bar but "struggle to meet" it for lack of time and money - a resources-vs-ideal gap, not a class divide in the ideal

## Method and identification

- Hays: interpretive/qualitative - in-depth interviews with mothers plus analysis of childcare advice literature; names the ideology, does not estimate prevalence
- Ishizuka: randomised vignette experiment, ~3,642 parents; parenting style, parent gender and child gender are experimentally varied, so class/gender differences in the RATING are identified cleanly and isolated from behaviour and resources

## Key takeaways for the model

- Parameterise a rising PARENTING-COST / quality bar per child (H373/H374): the norm inflates the perceived cost and the fear of inadequacy, pushing quantum down - a cultural cost-multiplier, not a cash price
- The bar is near-universal (~75% endorse intensive; only ~32‒38% accept low-intensity) and class-independent in the IDEAL - model it as a broad norm shock with resource-gated realisation, lower-resource households hitting a harder affordability wall
- Gender-symmetric: the cost falls on fathers and mothers alike, so it interacts with the second-shift / gender-equity levers rather than loading only on mothers
- Classification: INHERENT-CULTURAL norm - a diffuse standard policy cannot legislate down directly; it raises the price of the "ideal-parent" default rather than acting through one fiscal channel
