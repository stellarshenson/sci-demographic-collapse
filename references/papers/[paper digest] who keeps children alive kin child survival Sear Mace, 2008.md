## Summary

The foundational quantitative review of the cooperative-breeding hypothesis in humans: the claim that mothers do not raise children alone but depend on a wider kin network of helpers. The authors assemble 45 studies (mostly natural-fertility populations, historical and contemporary, across many regions) that test whether the presence of a given relative improves a child's survival probability, holding other factors constant.

## Key findings

- **In almost all 45 studies, at least one relative besides the mother measurably improves child survival** - humans do behave as cooperative breeders
- **Mother's presence is near-universal in importance**: her death is catastrophic for child survival at young ages
- **Maternal grandmother improves child survival in 69% of cases (9 of 13 studies; 7 of 11 among statistically valid studies)** - the single most reliable non-parental helper
- Paternal grandmothers help less consistently than maternal grandmothers; father's presence and paternal kin are the least consistent (fathers' contribution is often economic rather than directly protective)
- **Elder siblings ("helpers at the nest") also improve younger children's survival** in several populations, though the evidence is thinner
- Kin effects survive statistical controls for household economic status - the helper effect is not just a wealth proxy

## Relevance to this project

Grounds the mechanism half of the kin-support hypothesis: kin (especially maternal grandmothers and elder siblings) demonstrably lower the *cost and risk* of raising a child. But the outcome measured is child *survival*, not the *fertility rate* - the mechanism is strongest in high-mortality natural-fertility settings and does not by itself establish that kin availability raises completed fertility in a low-mortality, low-fertility country. For the model this argues that a kin channel, if added, belongs on the cost/security side (lowering the `S`-mediated suppression of childbearing) rather than as a direct multiplier on quantum, and that its force in modern low-mortality regions is an open, weaker question the review does not settle.
