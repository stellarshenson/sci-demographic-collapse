## Key mechanism

Bernardi and Klärner review how the fertility of the people AROUND an individual feeds back into that individual's own childbearing, and organise the evidence into four distinct social mechanisms. Social LEARNING: people acquire information about the costs, benefits and practicalities of parenthood by observing and talking to peers (reducing uncertainty). Social INFLUENCE / contagion: direct pressure to conform plus the emotional pull of seeing friends and siblings have children, shifting one's own intentions and timing. Social PRESSURE / control: normative sanctioning, obligations and expectations from kin and community that reward or punish (non)childbearing. Social SUPPORT: material and emotional help (childcare, money, reassurance) that lowers the real cost of a birth. The paper's key move is that these are separable channels acting on both the TIMING (tempo) and the NUMBER (quantum) of children, and that their strength is contingent on the STRUCTURE of the network (density, homogeneity, who is connected to whom), not universal.

## Main findings

- Four mechanisms are distinguished: social learning, social influence/contagion, social pressure/control, and social support - each with different content, and each documented to shift beliefs, norms and behaviour
- All four influence both fertility TEMPO (timing of births) and QUANTUM (completed family size), but their measured impact "strongly depends" on network structure and social context - there is no fixed effect size
- Peer/contagion effects are strongest in low- and late-fertility Western contexts, where childbearing is a deliberate, uncertainty-laden decision and peers supply scarce information and reassurance
- The effect is typically time-limited and interaction-based: influence peaks around a peer's own transition to parenthood and works through informal conversation with relatives, friends and colleagues
- Network structure is itself a variable: dense, homogeneous networks amplify pressure and support; sparse or heterogeneous ones dilute them - so the same mechanism can bend fertility up or leave it flat depending on topology

## Method and identification

- Narrative review / theoretical synthesis, not a single dataset; integrates ethnographic, panel and simulation studies of fertility diffusion (drawing on Granovetter's embeddedness framing)
- Identification is conceptual: the value is in separating four co-occurring channels that empirical studies usually bundle, and in flagging that "network effect" estimates are structure-dependent, not transportable
- Explicitly cautions that observed peer correlations mix genuine influence with shared context and selection into similar networks

## Key takeaways for the model

- Do NOT collapse "social influence on fertility" into one channel: model learning, influence/contagion, pressure and support as SEPARATE terms, since they respond to different levers and can compete (H368/H369)
- The tempo-vs-quantum split maps directly onto the campaign's Bongaarts-Feeney tempo term vs the quantum needs-coupling: peer contagion moves timing readily but completed number less so
- Effect magnitude is a FUNCTION of network structure (density/homogeneity), which justifies coupling the diffusion strength to the clustering/SCI terms from Centola and Bailey rather than using a fixed peer-effect constant
