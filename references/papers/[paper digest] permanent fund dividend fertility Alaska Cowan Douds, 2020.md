## Summary

Uses the Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend (an unconditional per-resident cash transfer paid since 1982, with newborns eligible) as a natural experiment on whether income shocks raise fertility. Identification via synthetic control and difference-in-differences.

## Key findings

- The APFD raised the fertility rate of women aged 15-44 by ~13.1%
- Effect concentrated in the 20-44 age group; also reduced birth spacing (a tempo/timing shift, not only quantum)
- Consistent with a positive-income-shock channel: financial readiness matters, and part of the response is faster birth timing
- The dividend scales with household size (larger households receive more), reinforcing the pro-natal income signal

## Relevance to this project

Grounds H160: an unconditional cash transfer does move births, and a meaningful share of the measured jump is tempo (earlier/closer-spaced births) rather than a permanent lift in completed fertility. Supports treating cash as a real but partly timing-shifted lever - the extensive-margin quantum gain is smaller than the headline percentage suggests.
