## Key mechanism

Uses the gender-specific component of the China trade shock as an exogenous test of the Wilson hypothesis: rising import competition falls disproportionately on male-intensive manufacturing, cutting young men's relative earnings and employment. Per Becker's model of household specialisation, when the high-earning spouse's (typically male) economic stature falls, the gains from marriage shrink, so marriage and fertility both drop - a clean causal identification of male economic prospects → family formation.

## Main findings

- A one-unit trade shock reduces births by 2.0 per 1,000 women (18-39); decomposed, a shock to male-intensive employment cuts fertility by 6.1 per 1,000 (t = -4.8), while a shock to female-intensive employment raises it by 2.5 - fertility tracks male, not female, earnings
- A shock to male-intensive employment reduces the fraction of young women ever-married by 4.2 points (12% of the 34.8% 1990 base) and currently-married by 3.6 points; female-intensive shocks move marriage the opposite way, two-thirds as large
- Trade shocks raise male idleness, absence, and premature mortality; excess male-versus-female mortality among ages 20-39 rises ~74-81 per 100K per decade, driven by drug/alcohol, liver disease and suicide - large against a ~1,200 per 100K baseline gap
- Because motherhood is less elastic than marriage, shocks raise the share of unwed mothers and of children in below-poverty, single-headed households (1990 child-poverty was 8.1% in married vs 47.4% in single-headed homes)

## Key takeaways

- Male economic prospects are causally load-bearing for marriage and fertility, and the effect is asymmetric - female earnings shocks do not substitute
- Erosion of "marriageable men" via a demand shock produces not just fewer marriages but a worse family-structure mix (unwed motherhood, single-parent poverty)
- The physical-decline channel (idleness, drug/alcohol mortality) is a downstream marker of the same lost-earnings shock, tightening the causal story
